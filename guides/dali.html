✨ Why Dali?

Dali is the China of postcards come to life: a walled old town backed by the snow-capped Cangshan Mountains, a giant blue lake on the other side, and white-walled Bai minority houses with ink paintings between every door. The pace is slow, the coffee is good, and the sunsets over Erhai Lake turn the whole sky pink.

But Dali isn't just pretty. For more than 500 years it was the capital of the independent Dali Kingdom, and the Bai people who still live here have kept their architecture, festivals, and food traditions alive. Whether you want to bike around the lake, learn tie-dye in a village workshop, or simply sit on a rooftop and watch the clouds roll over the mountains, Dali gives you permission to slow down.

The story: Erhai is a high-altitude freshwater lake shaped like an ear — "er" means ear in Chinese — sitting at 1,972 m between Dali Old Town and the Cangshan Mountains. For centuries it was the economic lifeline of the Dali Kingdom, feeding fishing villages and trading towns along its shore.

Why it's a must-see: The classic Dali experience is to rent an e-bike or join a car and trace the lakeshore, stopping at fishing villages, cafés, and viewpoints. Sunrise from the east shore (Caicun) is spectacular, and the water turns from silver to deep blue as the day warms up.

💡 Kat's tip: The full loop is 130 km — doable by e-bike in one long day if the battery is good, but most people split it. Check your rental's range. The west side near the old town gets crowded; the east side is quieter and better for sunrise.

The story: Dali Old Town dates back to the Ming dynasty (1382), when the city was rebuilt with a square wall, four main gates, and a grid of streets. For 500 years before that, it was the capital of the Dali Kingdom, which ruled much of today's Yunnan and Myanmar before Kublai Khan's Mongol armies conquered it in 1253.

Why it's a must-see: The old town is pedestrian-friendly, packed with white-walled Bai houses, stone streets, courtyard cafés, and rooftop bars with mountain views. Yes, it's touristy, but the core grid and city walls are genuinely ancient, and the people-watching is excellent.

💡 Kat's tip: Visit the South Gate at sunset for the classic gate-and-mountain photo. For a quieter experience, walk the side streets east of Foreigner Street (Yangren Jie) early in the morning.

The story: Zhoucheng, about 20 km north of Dali Old Town, is known as the "hometown of Chinese tie-dye." The Bai people here have been practicing indigo resist-dyeing for more than 1,000 years, using natural indigo from locally grown plants and hand-stitched patterns that create the signature blue-and-white designs.

Why it's a must-see: This is one of the best places in China to see a traditional craft still practiced in daily life. You can walk past workshops where women stitch patterns by hand, watch cloth being dipped into deep indigo vats, and even try making your own scarf or tote bag.

💡 Kat's tip: Skip the quick souvenir shops and look for a family workshop that offers a hands-on class. Allow 1–2 hours. Zhoucheng pairs perfectly with a morning visit to nearby Xizhou.

The story: Xizhou was a wealthy trading town on the Tea Horse Road, the ancient network that carried tea from Yunnan to Tibet and beyond. The Bai merchants who made their fortunes here built elaborate courtyard houses with grey-tiled roofs, marble inlays, and hand-painted gates — many of which still stand.

Why it's a must-see: Xizhou feels less commercial than Dali Old Town and gives you a clearer sense of Bai daily life. The morning market is busy and authentic, and several historic courtyards are open to visitors. The famous "Xizhou baba" (stuffed flatbread) is worth the trip alone.

💡 Kat's tip: Get there before 10am for the morning market. Try sweet and savory Xizhou baba from a street vendor, and visit the Linden Centre if it's open — it's a beautifully restored courtyard cultural center.

The story: The Three Pagodas of Chongsheng Temple are the symbol of Dali — three towers standing in a triangle, with the tallest reaching 70 meters. Built between the 9th and 10th centuries during the Nanzhao and Dali kingdoms, they survived earthquakes that destroyed everything around them. Behind them rise the 19 peaks of Cangshan Mountain, which reach over 4,000 meters.

Why it's a must-see: The Three Pagodas are the postcard view of Dali, and the temple complex behind them offers lake-and-mountain panoramas. Cangshan itself has hiking trails and a cable car up to 3,900 meters for those who want altitude and alpine views.

💡 Kat's tip: Visit the Three Pagodas in the morning for the best light on the mountains behind them. The Cangshan cable car can close in high wind — check conditions. For hikers, the Cloud Pass trail (Yunyou Road) between peaks is stunning.

🛏 Where to Stay

Pick the right district and half your trip is planned. These are the bases locals actually recommend for visitors.

Dali Ancient Town Best for first-timers

The classic choice: restaurants, cafés, and rooftop bars within the city walls, with easy access to buses and bike rentals. Best for travelers who want to be in the middle of things and don't mind some evening noise.

🚶 Walk to restaurants & shops 🚌 Buses to Erhai and villages
e.g. Jim's Tibetan Hotel (budget-mid) · boutique courtyard guesthouses
Caicun / Erhai East Shore Sunrise & lake views

A small village on the east side of Erhai with modern boutique hotels and cafés right on the water. Best for sunrise watchers, cyclists, and anyone who wants to wake up looking at the lake.

🌅 Best sunrise spot 🚲 Bike path along the shore
e.g. lakeside boutique hotels · ¥300–800/night
Xizhou Town Quiet & traditional

Stay in a restored Bai merchant courtyard 18 km north of Dali Old Town. Quieter, more authentic, and surrounded by rice fields. Best for travelers who want culture and calm over nightlife.

🏛 Historic Bai houses 🍳 Morning market nearby
e.g. Linden Centre · small courtyard guesthouses · ¥400–1200/night
Shuanglang Artistic & scenic

A fishing village turned artsy retreat on the east shore, about an hour's drive from Dali Old Town. Best for couples, photographers, and anyone who wants to do very little except look at the lake and mountains.

🚗 1 hour from old town 🎨 Art galleries & cafés
e.g. designer lake-view hotels · ¥500–1500/night

🍜 What to Eat

Bai sour & spicy fish 白族酸辣鱼

Fresh Erhai carp simmered with plums, chilies, and pickled vegetables. The broth is sour, spicy, and deeply savory — the signature dish of Bai cuisine.

🍽 Restaurants along Renmin Road in Dali Old Town · ¥60–120 per pot
Crossing-the-bridge noodles 过桥米线

Yes, it's a Kunming invention, but Dali has excellent versions too — hot chicken broth, rice noodles, and a tray of raw toppings that cook in seconds at your table.

🍽 Bridge-noodle shops on Huguo Road · ¥25–55 per set
Rose flower cake 鲜花饼

A flaky pastry filled with sweetened edible rose petals. Yunnan produces most of China's edible roses, and these cakes make perfect souvenirs.

🍽 Bakeries on Fuxing Road · ¥3–8 each
Xizhou baba 喜洲粑粑

A baked or fried stuffed flatbread from Xizhou, with savory minced pork or sweet rose-paste fillings. Crispy outside, soft inside, and eaten as breakfast or snack.

🍽 Street vendors in Xizhou · old town corners · ¥10–20

💡 Kat's Insider Tips

Kat Says
The UV at 2,000 m altitude is no joke. Wear sunscreen, sunglasses, and a hat even on cloudy days.
Kat Says
Don't try to bike the full 130 km Erhai loop on a weak e-bike. Rent one with confirmed long-range battery, or hire a car.
Kat Says
Zhoucheng Village is best in the morning. Pair it with Xizhou's morning market and Xizhou baba for a perfect half-day trip.
