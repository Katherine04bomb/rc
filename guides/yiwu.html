World's small-commodities market, crazy shopping

✨ Why Yiwu?

Yiwu is the most mind-bending shopping destination on earth: a wholesale market of 75,000 booths selling 2.1 million product types across 6.4 million square meters — certified by the UN and World Bank as the world's largest small-commodity market. If you spent 3 minutes at every booth, 8 hours a day, it would take over a year to see it all.

The market that started as a street-side stall line in 1982 is now the engine of global consumer goods — that $3 earring you bought anywhere in the world probably originated here. And since January 2026, a new high-speed link puts Yiwu just 33 minutes from Hangzhou. Come for the spectacle, stay for the street food, and leave with your luggage full of the world's cheapest souvenirs.

The story: From a 1982 street stall market, Yiwu has grown through six generations into the world's largest wholesale market: 6.4+ million square meters across five districts plus a new Digital Trade Center (opened October 2025). The UN, World Bank and Morgan Stanley have all called it the 'world's largest small-commodity market'; in 2024 it averaged 224,000 visitors a day, including 4,000+ foreign buyers from 230+ countries.

Why it's a must-see: Even if you never buy a thing, walking this market is jaw-dropping — it's a city of products, where every object on earth seems to have a booth. It's a national 4A tourist attraction, free to enter, and unlike any shopping experience you've had in your life.

💡 Kat's tip: Free entry, open 8:30am–5:30pm. Start at the tourist service center for a map. Most booths are wholesale-only (minimum orders apply), but many will sell a sample piece if you ask nicely. Bring a translator app — many vendors speak only Chinese.

The story: The oldest and busiest district (opened 2002, 340,000 m², 8,000+ booths): the ground floor sells artificial flowers, the second hair accessories and jewelry, the third crafts and festive decorations. It gathers over 80% of China's jewelry manufacturers, with 5,000+ new designs released weekly.

Why it's a must-see: This is where global fashion-accessory trends are born — if you've ever bought a $3 pair of earrings from a chain store anywhere in the world, they likely came from here. The sheer glittering density of merchandise is overwhelming in the best way.

💡 Kat's tip: Visit on weekday mornings for the fewest crowds. Look for booths with 'MOQ' (minimum order quantity) signs if you're actually sourcing; otherwise, it's a fascinating window-shopping spectacle.

The story: The second district (600,000 m², 8,000+ booths) covers bags and luggage, umbrellas, hardware tools, locks, small electronics and small appliances. The fourth floor hosts specialty pavilions offering factory-direct supply, and the district's bags-and-luggage zone alone feeds importers worldwide.

Why it's a must-see: This is where Amazon sellers and importers come to source suitcases, power tools and kitchen gadgets by the container load. Even as a casual visitor, seeing the full breadth of 'Made in China' in one building is eye-opening — and fun.

💡 Kat's tip: If you're sourcing, bring business cards and a notebook — vendors will quote MOQs and FOB prices on the spot. The fourth-floor regional pavilions are less crowded with higher-quality goods.

The story: District 3 (460,000 m²) concentrates stationery, office supplies, sports goods and cosmetics — its 1,900+ stationery vendors supply 100+ countries. District 4 (1.08 million m², 16,000+ booths) is the single largest district, selling socks, underwear, shoes and daily goods; its sock zone alone exports over 3 billion RMB a year.

Why it's a must-see: District 4 is so large it could be a city by itself — the world's biggest sock-trading hub, where you'll find socks printed with everything from anime characters to national flags, sold by the thousand-pair carton. It's a museum of daily life, in bulk.

💡 Kat's tip: District 4 is overwhelming — pick one category (socks on the lower floors, say) and focus. The buildings have internal elevated roads so delivery trucks drive right onto each floor; watch for vehicles in the corridors.

The story: District 5 (640,000 m², 7,000+ booths) focuses on imported goods, bedding and textiles, with a first-floor Import Pavilion stocking nearly 100,000 products from dozens of countries. The brand-new Digital Trade Center (opened October 2025, 1.25 million m²) is Yiwu's sixth-generation market: AI sourcing tools, livestream studios and virtual showrooms.

Why it's a must-see: District 5's Import Pavilion is the easiest place in China to buy authentic foreign products — Italian wine, Japanese cosmetics, Russian chocolate — at near-wholesale prices. The Digital Trade Center shows where the world's market is heading, and it's genuinely cool.

💡 Kat's tip: The Import Pavilion is the best place for tourist shopping — many items sell individually. The Digital Trade Center has a visitor experience zone with AI demos; 30 minutes is enough if you're not buying.

🛏 Where to Stay

Pick the right district and half your trip is planned. These are the bases locals actually recommend for visitors.

International Trade City area (Futian) Buyers & market visitors

The most practical base if the market is your goal: business hotels cluster around Futian Street, within a 5-minute walk of Districts 1–5, plus the Foreign Buyer Service Center and logistics offices.

🚇 Jin-Yi-Dong light rail — International Trade City Station 🏛 Yiwu International Trade City, Digital Trade Center
e.g. Hilton Yiwu (upscale, ~600 RMB) · Yiwu Mansion (mid-range, ~350 RMB)
Binwang / downtown Nightlife & food

The old center around Binwang Market and Choucheng Square has the best night markets and street food — plus Yiwu's famous Korean Quarter (the city hosts a large Korean trading community). 10 minutes by taxi from the Trade City.

🚇 Jin-Yi-Dong light rail — Yiwu Station; buses to Trade City 15 min 🏛 Binwang Night Market, Korean Quarter, Jiming Pavilion
e.g. Atour Hotel Binwang (~300 RMB) · Yiwu International Hotel (~280 RMB)
Fotang Ancient Town Culture seekers

About 35 minutes south of downtown, staying in Fotang lets you experience the ancient town at its most atmospheric — empty old streets at dawn, temple bells, river mist — with boutique guesthouses in renovated merchant houses.

🚇 No metro — bus 318 or taxi (~40 RMB) 🏛 Fotang Old Street, Wan Shan Floating Bridge, Water Street
e.g. Fotang Old Street minsu (~250 RMB) · local guesthouses (~120 RMB)

🍜 What to Eat

Fotang Qianzhang Noodles 佛堂千张面

Thin wheat noodles in a clear broth topped with shredded dried tofu skin and pork — a 100-year-old local breakfast staple. Simple, savory, deeply comforting.

🍽 🍽 Xinghua Noodle Shop, Fotang Old Street · ~13 RMB
Brown Sugar Twists 红糖麻花

Yiwu's brown-sugar industry dates back over 700 years (a national intangible heritage) — twists and pastries drenched in freshly boiled brown-sugar syrup. Crunchy, chewy, not too sweet.

🍽 🍽 Yihong Brown Sugar Hall (Fotang) & street vendors · ¥10–15
Korean BBQ 韩式烤肉

Yiwu's large Korean merchant community created an authentic Korean dining scene — grilled marinated short ribs, stone bibimbap and cold noodles that rival Seoul.

🍽 🍽 Korean Quarter near Binwang · ¥50–80 pp
Mixed Tofu Pudding 豆花

Silken tofu pudding, sweet (sugar syrup) or savory (soy sauce, chili oil, pickles) — a refreshing street snack, especially in summer.

🍽 🍽 Fotang old-street vendors & Gongshang Night Market · ¥3–5

💡 Kat's Insider Tips

Kat Says
The fastest train from Yiwu to Hangzhou East takes just 33 minutes (from ~45 RMB) — day-trip from Hangzhou, but the market deserves at least one full day, ideally two.
Kat Says
The market is not designed for retail tourists — most booths need minimum orders of 50–500 pieces. For single-item shopping, head to District 5's Import Pavilion or the tourist center on District 1's ground floor.
Kat Says
Download the 'Yiwu Go' (义乌购) app before visiting — it searches products and booth numbers, so you can navigate the 6.4-million-sqm complex without getting lost. Free Wi-Fi throughout.
