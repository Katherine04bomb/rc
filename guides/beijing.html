✨ Why Beijing?

Beijing is where China's story gets loud. For over 800 years — across six dynasties — emperors ruled from this city, and the evidence is everywhere: the world's largest palace complex, the Great Wall curling over the mountains an hour away, and temple roofs older than most countries. If you only visit one place in China, this is the one.

But Beijing isn't a museum. Ancient hutong lanes sit beside world-class art districts, morning tai chi in temple parks gives way to rooftop bars in Sanlitun, and the subway carries you from a 600-year-old palace to the futuristic Bird's Nest in minutes. History, food, and modern China — all in one city.

The story: The Great Wall isn't one wall but 20,000+ km of fortifications built over 2,000 years (3rd century BC to 17th century) by millions of workers. Mutianyu was first built in the Northern Qi dynasty (550 AD), then rebuilt in 1368 by Ming general Xu Da as the capital's northern shield. It's the longest fully-restored section open to visitors — 5.4 km with 23 watchtowers — and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987.

Why it's a must-see: Of all the famous sections near Beijing, Mutianyu gives you the most wall for the fewest crowds — fewer tourists than Badaling, forested mountain views, and original Ming bricks under your feet. Cable car up, wander the watchtowers, then ride the toboggan slide down. It's the most fun you'll ever have on a World Heritage site.

💡 Kat's tip: Go early (before 9am) and avoid weekends. It's about 1.5–2 hours from central Beijing — book a day tour or take the bus from Dongzhimen hub.

The story: Built 1406–1420 by the Yongle Emperor with over a million laborers, the Forbidden City (Zijincheng, "Purple Forbidden City") was home to 24 emperors of the Ming and Qing dynasties for nearly 500 years. It's the largest palace complex in the world: 72 hectares, 980 buildings, more than 8,700 rooms, surrounded by a 10-meter wall and a 52-meter moat. A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1987.

Why it's a must-see: Commoners were once forbidden to enter — now you can walk the same stones as emperors, from the Hall of Supreme Harmony with its Dragon Throne to the serene Imperial Garden. The whole complex is built around the emperor as the center of the universe: the throne is aligned with the North Star itself.

💡 Kat's tip: Tickets sell out days ahead — book online at least 7 days in advance. Enter from the Meridian Gate (south), then exit the north gate into Jingshan Park for the classic rooftop view.

The story: At 440,000 m² (880m × 500m), Tiananmen Square is the largest city square in the world — big enough to hold 60 football fields. It sits on Beijing's historic central axis, flanked by the National Museum of China, the Great Hall of the People, and the Monument to the People's Heroes, with the Forbidden City rising directly behind it.

Why it's a must-see: Come at sunrise for the flag-raising ceremony, timed to the sun's first light each morning. Thousands of locals gather to watch — it's a daily ritual that turns an enormous concrete square into something deeply moving, and it makes China feel real rather than postcard-perfect.

💡 Kat's tip: Entry is free, but bring your passport — security checks are strict. Arrive 30 minutes early for a good view; the ceremony on the 1st of each month is bigger with a full band.

The story: Built in 1420 by the same Yongle Emperor, the Temple of Heaven is where Ming and Qing emperors came each year to pray for good harvests — the largest ritual complex of its kind in the world (273 hectares). Its layout is round in the north and square in the south, echoing the ancient belief that "heaven is round, earth is square." A UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1998.

Why it's a must-see: The Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests is a wooden masterpiece — built without a single nail, its blue-tiled roof held up by interlocking brackets. But the real magic is the park itself: come in the morning and you'll find locals doing tai chi, ballroom dancing, singing opera, and playing cards. It's Beijing's best free show.

💡 Kat's tip: Go early (6–9am) — that's when the park becomes the city's living room and the exercise culture is in full swing. The park opens at 6am.

The story: Built in 1750 by Emperor Qianlong (originally as a birthday gift for his mother), the Summer Palace — Yiheyuan, "Garden of Nurtured Harmony" — was rebuilt by Empress Dowager Cixi in 1888–95 after foreign forces sacked it in 1860. It's the best-preserved imperial garden in China and a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1998.

Why it's a must-see: A 290-hectare landscape wrapped around Kunming Lake, with the world's longest painted corridor (728 meters, 14,000+ paintings), the Marble Boat, and the Seventeen-Arch Bridge. Take a dragon boat across the lake and you'll understand how emperors relaxed — and why they called it the "summer" palace.

💡 Kat's tip: Give it half a day. Rent a boat if the weather is fine, and stay for sunset from the Seventeen-Arch Bridge — it's worth the wait.

🛏 Where to Stay

Pick the right district and half your trip is planned. These are the four bases locals actually recommend for visitors.

Dongcheng / Wangfujing Best for first-timers

The classic choice: walk to the Forbidden City in 10–15 minutes and Tiananmen in 20, with Metro Line 1 at Wangfujing station linking you to everything else. Shopping, restaurants, and the famous snack street are right outside your door.

🚇 Line 1 · Wangfujing 🏛 Walk to Forbidden City
e.g. Happy Dragon Courtyard (budget, near Forbidden City) · The Beijing Hotel (landmark)
Qianmen & Dashilan Old Beijing on a budget

South of Tiananmen (15–20 minutes on foot), this was the Qing dynasty's merchant district. Restored shopping streets, hutong courtyards, and cheap eats everywhere — plus budget hotels from ¥150–300 a night. Best for budget travelers and food lovers.

🚇 Line 2 · Qianmen 🏮 Qing-era streets & snacks
e.g. courtyard guesthouses behind Dashilan · ¥150–300/night
Hutong courtyards (Shichahai / Nanluoguxiang) The experience splurge

Sleep in a centuries-old siheyuan (courtyard home): gray brick walls, wooden lattice windows, birds in the morning. A 20–25 minute metro ride from the Forbidden City. Best for couples and anyone who wants to actually feel old Beijing.

🚇 Line 2/8 · Gulou Dajie 🌳 Lakeside hutongs
e.g. The Orchid Hotel (boutique hutong stay) · ¥500–1500/night
Sanlitun / Chaoyang Modern Beijing

Beijing's nightlife and foodie hub — international restaurants, bars, and the Taikoo Li mall, with the 798 Art District a short ride away. Best for repeat visitors, foodies, and night owls. Trade-off: 30–35 minutes to the Forbidden City by metro.

🚇 Line 10 · Tuanjiehu 🎨 798 Art District nearby
e.g. NUO Hotel Beijing (luxury near 798)

🍜 What to Eat

Peking Duck 北京烤鸭

Crispy lacquered skin sliced tableside, wrapped in thin pancakes with scallion, cucumber, and sweet bean sauce. The locals' favorite — better value and less touristy than the old famous names.

🍽 Siji Minfu · ≈¥150–280 per duck · book ahead
Zhajiangmian 炸酱面

Beijing's everyday bowl: thick wheat noodles tossed in fermented soybean-and-pork sauce, topped with cucumber and bean sprouts. Hearty, salty, and cheap — the taste of a hutong lunch.

🍽 Noodle shops around Qianmen & the hutongs · ¥20–45
Jianbing 煎饼

A crispy egg crêpe folded around cracker, scallion, and sauce — the city's street-breakfast ritual, cooked fresh in two minutes flat. Grab one before a day of sightseeing.

🍽 Any breakfast cart with a line of locals · ¥8–18
Copper-pot mutton hot pot 涮羊肉

Thin-sliced lamb cooked in clear broth over a copper chimney pot, dipped in rich sesame sauce. Beijing's winter soul food — warm, social, and deeply satisfying.

🍽 Dong Lai Shun · traditional spots around Niujie · ¥100–220 pp
