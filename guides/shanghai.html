✨ Why Shanghai?

Shanghai is the easiest place in China for a first-timer — English is everywhere, the metro runs like clockwork, and almost every app you need works here. But don't mistake easy for boring: for over a century this has been China's most international city, a place where a 1920s French villa sits around the corner from a 600-meter supertall skyscraper.

The Bund gives you the world's greatest skyline show for free, the old French Concession is made for slow afternoons and coffee, and the food scene runs from street-side soup dumplings to Michelin-starred tasting menus. If you only have a week in China, spend it here — and if you have more, Shanghai is the perfect first stop before the rest of the country.

The story: The Bund is a 1.5-km strip of 52 colonial-era buildings from the 1920s–30s — banks and trading houses built by British, French, American and Japanese firms when Shanghai was the 'Paris of the East'. Across the Huangpu River, Pudong's skyline rose from farmland in the 1990s: the Oriental Pearl Tower (1994), the 492-m Shanghai World Financial Center (2008) and the 632-m Shanghai Tower (2015), China's tallest building.

Why it's a must-see: It's the only place on earth where you can stand in front of century-old European architecture and look directly at a fully futuristic Chinese skyline — the past and the future of Shanghai in a single glance. Come at night when the towers light up, and again at dawn when it's just you, the river, and the city waking up.

💡 Kat's tip: The Bund is free and open 24/7. Go at 7pm for the full light show, then walk two blocks back into Nanjing Road for street food. For the classic postcard view of the Bund itself, cross to Pudong and look back from Lujiazui.

The story: Yu Garden was built between 1559 and 1577 by Pan Yunduan, a Ming-dynasty official, as a private garden for his parents — 'Yu' means 'peace and comfort'. It's a masterpiece of classical Chinese garden design: rockeries, carp ponds, zigzag bridges and dragon walls that hide the garden's true size (about 2 hectares) behind every turn.

Why it's a must-see: It's the finest example in Shanghai of a classical Chinese garden — the kind of delicate pavilions-and-ponds scene you see in paintings. Around it, the old city walls are gone but the old-city lanes survive: tiny shops selling tea, jade and calligraphy brushes, all squeezed under traditional roofs.

💡 Kat's tip: Arrive at opening (9am) to beat the crowds. Right outside the gates, the snack street Nanxiang serves the legendary xiaolongbao — get the crab-roe version. Garden entry is 40 RMB.

The story: From 1849 to 1943 this leafy district was the French Concession, and it still feels European — wide avenues shaded by 100-year-old plane trees, alleys of 1920s townhouses, and some of Shanghai's most beautiful streets (Wukang Road is the star). It was home to artists, writers and spies; Sun Yat-sen and Soong Ching-ling both lived here.

Why it's a must-see: This is the Shanghai locals choose for weekend strolls: coffee on every corner, tiny galleries, vintage shops and tree-dappled streets with zero skyscrapers. It's the closest China gets to a Parisian afternoon, and the best place to slow down after the Bund's sensory overload.

💡 Kat's tip: Rent a shared bike (Meituan or HelloBike, ~1.5 RMB per 30 min) and wander without a map — get lost on purpose. Wukang Road, Anfu Road and Tianzifang's alleys are the highlights. Allow half a day.

The story: Founded in 1952, the Shanghai Museum moved in 1996 into a striking round-building-with-square-base design that echoes the ancient Chinese belief 'heaven is round, earth is square'. Its collection of over one million pieces is ranked among the best in China, with world-class galleries of bronzes, ceramics, calligraphy, jade and Ming-Qing furniture.

Why it's a must-see: Chinese museums rarely offer this combination: world-class artifacts, English labels, free entry, and air conditioning when the summer heat hits. The bronze gallery alone — ritual vessels up to 3,000 years old — is worth the trip. It's the perfect rainy-day or escape-the-heat move.

💡 Kat's tip: Entry is free but you must reserve online (WeChat mini-program or official site) with your passport — book a few days ahead in high season. Allow 2–3 hours; the bronze and ceramic galleries are the unmissable ones.

The story: Tianzifang is a warren of shikumen (stone-gate) lane houses from the 1930s that artists began moving into around 1998, turning it into Shanghai's most creative quarter. Today its alleys hide hundreds of tiny studios, galleries, craft shops, cafés and bars — a living, working neighborhood rather than a reconstructed 'old town'.

Why it's a must-see: Unlike polished tourist streets elsewhere, Tianzifang feels real — residents still hang laundry above the shops, and behind every doorway is something handmade and unexpected. It's the most atmospheric place in Shanghai to shop for gifts, watch artisans work, and duck into a laneway bar at night.

💡 Kat's tip: Go on a weekday (weekends are shoulder-to-shoulder). The alleys are a maze — pick one direction and just wander. For the classic view, climb to one of the rooftop bars around the edge of the maze.

🛏 Where to Stay

Pick the right district and half your trip is planned. These are the bases locals actually recommend for visitors.

The Bund / Nanjing Road First-timers & skyline views

Wake up to the Pudong skyline from your window, stroll the Bund at dawn before the crowds, and step out your door onto Nanjing Road — Shanghai's famous shopping street. Metro Line 2 and 10 run right through, and the old city with Yu Garden is a 15-minute walk.

🚇 Line 2/10 · East Nanjing Road 🏛 The Bund, Nanjing Road
e.g. Les Suites Orient (mid-range, river views) · Peace Hotel (legendary art-deco landmark)
People's Square / Huangpu Central & convenient

The dead-center of Shanghai: Shanghai Museum on one side, the old city and Yu Garden to the south, and the French Concession a 10-minute taxi ride away. Two major metro lines cross here, and prices are more reasonable than the Bund.

🚇 Lines 1/2/8 · People's Square 🏛 Shanghai Museum, Yu Garden, Nanjing Road
e.g. JW Marriott Shanghai at Tomorrow Square (landmark) · budget chains around the square
French Concession (Xuhui / Jing'an) Café & architecture lovers

The most atmospheric place to stay — tree-lined streets, boutiques and coffee shops, and the city's best brunch scene. It's less central for sightseeing but perfect if you want to feel the real Shanghai rhythm. Wukang Road's historic mansions are right outside.

🚇 Lines 1/7/10/11 · Changshu Rd / Shanghai Library 🏛 Wukang Road, Tianzifang, Anfu Road
e.g. The Puli Hotel (luxury, Jing'an) · Capella Shanghai (restored shikumen villas, splurge)
Lujiazui (Pudong) Modern skyline & business

Stay inside the skyline you came to see: the financial district where the Shanghai Tower and Oriental Pearl live. Prices are high, and crossing the river adds 20 minutes to the Bund, but the night views from a high floor are unbeatable.

🚇 Line 2 · Lujiazui 🏛 Shanghai Tower, Oriental Pearl, IFC Mall
e.g. Grand Hyatt Shanghai (tower views) · Mandarin Oriental Pudong (luxury)

🍜 What to Eat

Xiaolongbao 小笼包

Delicate steamed dumplings holding hot pork-and-gelatin broth inside. The Shanghai ritual: bite a small hole, slurp the soup, then eat the rest with vinegar and ginger.

🍽 🍽 Jia Jia Tang Bao (Michelin-mentioned) · Yu Garden snack street · ¥15–30 per basket
Shengjianbao 生煎包

Pan-fried pork buns with a crispy golden bottom, juicy filling and a burst of hot soup — xiaolongbao's heartier, crunchier cousin, and the true local breakfast.

🍽 🍽 Da Hu Chun (legendary since 1932) · ¥12–20 per order of 4
Hairy Crab 大闸蟹

A seasonal obsession (Oct–Nov only): small lake crabs prized for their creamy roe and sweet meat. Locals plan entire dinners around them — worth scheduling your trip for.

🍽 🍽 Wang Bao He on Fuzhou Road · ¥80–200 per crab in season · book ahead
Scallion Oil Noodles 葱油拌面

Springy noodles tossed with scallion-infused oil and soy — deceptively simple, deeply comforting, and the taste of a Shanghai home kitchen.

🍽 🍽 Any local canteen or noodle shop · ¥15–25

💡 Kat's Insider Tips

Kat Says
Get a Shanghai metro card or just tap with your phone (Alipay/WeChat Pay) — the network is huge, cheap (~3–6 RMB) and fully in English. Avoid taxis during rush hour.
Kat Says
The Bund at night and the Bund at 7am are two completely different experiences — do both. Night for the lights, dawn for the colonial architecture and the tai chi crowd.
Kat Says
Almost everything in Shanghai can be paid with Alipay or WeChat Pay; for the small old-city snack stalls carry 50–100 RMB in cash as backup.
