Lhasa

Potala Palace, Tibetan Buddhism, high-altitude magic

★★★★☆ 🗓 4–5 days High altitudeTibetan cultureMonasteries

✨ Why Lhasa?

At 3,650 meters above sea level, Lhasa — "the Place of the Gods" in Tibetan — is one of the highest cities on Earth and the beating heart of Tibetan Buddhism. The Potala Palace crowns the skyline like nothing else in China, and the air itself seems thinner, clearer, and more alive. This is a city you feel in your lungs and your soul.

Time moves differently here. Pilgrims walk the Barkhor circuit spinning prayer wheels, monks argue philosophy in monastery courtyards, and buttery incense drifts from every shrine. The Qinghai–Tibet railway and a new high-altitude airport make it reachable, but nothing about Lhasa feels easy or ordinary. Come for the once-in-a-lifetime views; stay for the quiet awe.

The story: The Potala rises 13 storeys and 117 meters above Lhasa, built from 1645 onward as the winter palace of the Dalai Lamas. It holds more than 1,000 rooms, 10,000 shrines, and the gilded tombs of eight Dalai Lamas. For 300 years it was the seat of Tibetan government and the most recognizable building on the Tibetan plateau.

Why it's a must-see: Because there is nothing else on Earth like it. The white walls are palaces and offices, the red center is the sacred halls, and inside, corridors of butter lamps and tapestries lead to jewel-encrusted stupas. The view of the city from the roof is unforgettable.

💡 Kat's tip: Book tickets online two days ahead — numbers are strictly limited and it sells out fast. Go early (morning slots) when the light hits the white walls. Give it 3 hours; the interior is a maze.

The story: The Jokhang, built in the 7th century, houses the Jowo Shakyamuni — a statue of Buddha at age 12 that Tibetans believe is the most sacred object in the world. Since it was placed here, pilgrims have walked the Barkhor circuit around the temple clockwise, spinning prayer wheels and touching foreheads to the stones.

Why it's a must-see: This is Tibet's spiritual core, more profound than any sight. Join the flow of pilgrims on Barkhor Street at dawn — some in fur hats, some prostrating full-length in the dust. The incense, the murmur of prayers, the golden rooftops: it's overwhelming in the best way.

💡 Kat's tip: Walk the Barkhor circuit clockwise — it's a sacred rule, never counter-clockwise. Enter the Jokhang early (8am) to avoid queues. Dress modestly and remove hats indoors.

The story: Founded in 1419, Sera is one of the "Big Three" Gelug monasteries of Lhasa. Its fame today rests on a daily ritual that looks like an argument — from 3pm, monks gather in a gravel courtyard and debate Buddhist philosophy with dramatic claps, slaps, and shouts of "Dhi!"

Why it's a must-see: The debates are genuinely fascinating theater: one monk sits, one stands and attacks his logic with physical gestures, and the clap is meant to "wake the mind." You don't need to understand Tibetan to feel the energy. The monastery's rooftop views over the valley are a bonus.

💡 Kat's tip: Time your visit for the 3pm debates (daily except Sundays in winter). It's 20 minutes by taxi from central Lhasa. Combine with a walk through the old quarters below the monastery.

The story: In its 17th-century heyday, Drepung — "Rice Heap" — housed 10,000 monks and was the largest monastery on Earth, ranking above the Potala as the seat of the Dalai Lamas' government. Its whitewashed buildings sprawl across the hillside like a small white city.

Why it's a must-see: It's the grandest monastery complex in Tibet and largely untouristed compared to the Potala. Climb through silent lanes, butter-lamp halls, and courtyards where monks still live and study. The main assembly hall holds 5,000 seated monks at once.

💡 Kat's tip: Combine it with Sera for a full monastery day — Drepung in the morning, Sera in the afternoon. The hike up the hill is steep; take the shuttle or a taxi if the altitude bothers you.

The story: Namtso means "Heavenly Lake" in Tibetan. At 4,718 meters and 1,920 square kilometers, it's the highest large saltwater lake in the world — a sheet of impossibly turquoise water under the Nyenchen Tanglha snow range. Tibetans revere it as a holy lake; pilgrims circle its 280-km shore on foot.

Why it's a must-see: This is the classic full-day trip from Lhasa and the most dramatic scenery you can reach in one day. The lake shifts from sapphire to jade with the clouds, yaks graze on the shore, and prayer flags snap in the wind. Sunset here is a religious experience.

💡 Kat's tip: The altitude is brutal — acclimatize in Lhasa for at least two days first. Go with a driver or day tour (4 hours each way). Bring layers; it's freezing even in summer. Don't stay overnight if you're sensitive to altitude.

🛏 Where to Stay

Pick the right district and half your trip is planned. These are the bases locals actually recommend for visitors.

Barkhor / Old Town Best for atmosphere

Lhasa's old Tibetan quarter: narrow lanes, courtyards, and the pilgrim circuit outside your door. Traditional guesthouses with courtyards and rooftop views of the Potala. Best for culture and atmosphere.

🚶 Walk to Jokhang Temple 🏮 Traditional guesthouses
e.g. heritage courtyard inns near Barkhor · Shambhala Guesthouse
Potala View / West Best for first-timers

Modern hotels facing the Potala Palace, many with rooftop or window views of the red-and-white icon. Quieter, more comfortable, and a short walk to the palace entrance. Best for comfort and views.

🚶 Walk to Potala Palace 🏔 Palace-facing rooms
e.g. St. Regis Lhasa · Himalaya Hotel (palace views)
North / Lhasa River Modern & quiet

The newer northern quarter along the Lhasa River: wider streets, modern cafés, and fewer tourists. Best for longer stays, remote work, or travelers who want oxygen bars and modern amenities.

🍜 What to Eat

Sweet tea 甜茶

Milk tea brewed with black tea, sugar, and yak milk — served in thermos bottles at every teahouse. Tibetans sit for hours over sweet tea, chatting and watching the street. It's also the best cure for altitude headaches (caffeine + hydration).

🍽 Teahouses on Barkhor Street · ¥3–5 per cup
Tibetan noodles 藏面

Hand-pulled wheat noodles in a simple broth with minced yak meat and scallions, eaten for breakfast with a side of pickled vegetables. Hearty, warming, and cheap.

🍽 Morning noodle shops near Barkhor · ¥10–20
Momo dumplings 藏式饺子

Tibet's answer to dumplings: thick-skinned, steamed or fried, stuffed with yak meat, vegetables, or cheese. Served with a fierce chili sauce. Steamed momo with sweet tea is the classic Tibetan lunch.

🍽 Restaurants across the Old Town · ¥15–30
Yak hot pot 牦牛火锅

Thin-sliced yak meat cooked in a bubbling broth with mushrooms and greens — the classic high-altitude dinner. Yak meat is lean, rich, and tastes like nowhere else. Finish with butter tea if you're brave.

🍽 Hot pot restaurants near Beijing Road · ¥80–150 pp
